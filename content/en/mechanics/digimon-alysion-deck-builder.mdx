The real reason is redundancy. You do not win because your best card is powerful; you win because your best card shows up often enough to matter. The math below uses a 50-card deck and a 7-card opening hand as a working example — adjust for your format's actual limits, but the shape of the curve stays the same.

| Copies in deck | Chance of seeing at least one in your opening hand |
|---|---|
| 1 copy | ~14% |
| 2 copies | ~26% |
| 3 copies | ~37% |
| 4 copies | ~46% |

That table explains the single most common deckbuilding rule in card games: anything you want to see every game should be at maximum copies, and anything you want only occasionally stays at one or two. A "flex" card you slot in as a single copy is not a plan — it is a lottery ticket. ## Core Ratios: Slot-by-Slot Counts That Hold Up

Before you argue about individual cards, decide what job each slot does. Decks fail most often because they are missing a role entirely, not because one card is slightly suboptimal. The starting points below are community-reported ranges that hold up across most aggressive and midrange shells; treat them as a first draft, then tune.

| Role | Starting count | What it does | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core engine / starter pieces | 8–12 | Gets your game plan moving early | You frequently pass turn one with nothing to do |
| Primary attacker line | 12–16 | Converts advantage into pressure | You stabilize but cannot close |
| Interaction and removal | 8–12 | Answers opposing threats | You lose to a single resolved threat |
| Search, draw, and filtering | 6–8 | Turns draws into the cards you need | You run out of gas after two exchanges |
| Flex and tech slots | 4–6 | Covers bad matchups | These creep past 8 and dilute the plan |

A useful sanity check: cover the right-hand column and ask whether your list has an answer for each warning sign. If three or more apply, the deck is not ready for testing — it is ready for another pass in the Digimon Alysion deck builder.

### Splashing a Second Color

Splashing is tempting and usually worth it, but only under three conditions:

- Your splash cards win games on their own, not just fill curve gaps.
- Your search and filtering can actually find the splash cards.
- The splash does not push your average cost up enough to cause early stumbles.

If any of those is false, cut the splash and tighten the main plan. A focused deck with a clear identity beats a deck that answers everything badly.

## Cost Curve and Consistency: Avoiding the Dead-Hand Problem

Curve is the quiet killer. A deck can have perfect ratios and still lose because it draws three expensive cards it cannot play and two cheap cards that do nothing. The Digimon Alysion deck builder displays average cost alongside your totals, and that single number is one of the fastest diagnostics available: if your average is climbing while your cheap interaction is shrinking, you have built a deck that only functions when it draws perfectly.

| Cost bracket | Rough target share | If you run too many | If you run too few |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cheap, early plays | 30–40% | Low impact late, but keeps you alive | You fall behind before your plan starts |
| Mid-cost core | 40–50% | Fine, but watch for clumping | Your engine never comes online |
| High-cost finishers | 10–20% | Dead cards in the opening hand | You cannot convert a lead into a win |
| Situational / reactive | 5–15% | Inconsistent, matchup dependent | No answers when you need them |

Three quick checks before you call a list finished:

- **Opening hand test:** deal yourself ten opening hands. If four or more are unplayable, the curve is wrong.
- **Top-deck test:** imagine the game is even and you are empty-handed. How many cards in your deck would you be happy to draw? Aim for at least 60%.
- **Cost density test:** count how many cards you can play on your first two turns. Fewer than eight is a red flag in most formats.

The Digimon Alysion deck builder's type and cost breakdown panels make all three checks a two-minute exercise rather than a full evening of shuffling.

## A Testing Routine That Turns Lists Into Winners

Theory only gets you to a first draft. The gap between a list that looks good and a deck that performs is closed by structured testing, and the tool supports that directly — export your list as text, share it with a playtest partner, re-import their version, and compare notes. | Stage | Games | Focus | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo goldfish | 10 | Can the deck execute its own plan? | Plan completes in 7 of 10 games |
| Mirror / same archetype | 5 | Who is faster and more consistent? | You win the resource race more often than not |
| Aggro matchup | 5 | Can you survive the early game? | At least 3 wins or 3 close losses |
| Control matchup | 5 | Can you close before running out of gas? | You force a decision point by mid-game |
| Sideboard / tech swap | 5 | Do your flex slots actually change outcomes? | Each tech card wins at least one game |

Track more than wins and losses. The metrics below tell you *why* a list is underperforming, which is far more useful than a scoreline.

| Metric | How to measure | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Cards seen per game | Count draws plus searches | Under 12 means your engine is too thin |
| Turns to first real threat | Note the turn you apply pressure | Turn 5+ in an aggressive shell |
| Games lost to a dead hand | Flag hands with no playable card | More than 1 in 5 |
| Flex slot impact | Note when each tech card mattered | A tech card that never mattered in 10 games |

## Common Deckbuilding Mistakes the Digimon Alysion Deck Builder Exposes

Most failed lists fail in predictable ways, and the Digimon Alysion deck builder surfaces all of them if you know where to look. The table below pairs each mistake with the symptom you will actually notice during testing.

| Mistake | Symptom in play | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too many one-of "pet" cards | Deck feels different every game | Cut to the best 2–3 and raise copies |
| Ignoring the average cost readout | Frequent unplayable opening hands | Swap two expensive cards for cheap interaction |
| Over-splashing colors | Search effects miss, curve rises | Reduce to a single splash or drop it |
| No dedicated finisher | You stabilize, then lose | Add 3–4 cards that close games |
| Building from memory | Counts drift, ratios break | Rebuild the list inside the tool, not on paper |
| Skipping the export step | No record of what you tested | Save and export every version before changes |

One habit separates improving brewers from stuck ones: version control. Save each iteration, name it, and export the text list before you change anything. When a change makes the deck worse — and some will — you can revert in seconds instead of trying to remember what the list looked like two hours ago. Community reports are full of players who "fixed" a deck into oblivion because they had no record of the version that worked.

## FAQ

**Is the Digimon Alysion deck builder required to build a competitive deck?**
No, but it removes the guesswork. Counting roles, checking your average cost, and exporting lists for review are the habits that separate consistent decks from lucky ones, and the tool automates almost all of it.

**How many copies of a key card should I run?**
Start at maximum copies for anything central to your plan, then drop to two or three for situational cards. Remember the math: a single copy appears in your opening hand roughly 14% of the time in a 50-card example, so one-ofs are not reliable plans.

**How long should I test before changing my list?**
Give a version at least ten games before making structural changes, and five games before small tweaks. Changing three cards after two losses tells you nothing except that you lost twice.

**Does the Digimon Alysion deck builder help with sharing decks?**
Yes — export your list as text, send it to a playtest partner, and import their version to compare builds side by side. Sharing lists is the fastest way to catch a role you forgot to include.
